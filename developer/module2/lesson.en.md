# Module 2 — From Lovelace to Turing

The idea that a machine can follow instructions isn't self-evident.
Someone had to come up with it. And before that, someone had to dare to think it.

This is the story of the people who did that — long before there was ever a screen.

## 1. Charles Babbage — the machine that was never finished (1820s)

Charles Babbage was a British mathematician with an obsession: errors in calculation tables.

In his time, mathematical tables — for shipping, artillery, taxes — were calculated by hand, by people. And people make mistakes. Babbage wanted a machine that didn't.

He designed the **Difference Engine**: a mechanical calculator of gears and levers. And later the **Analytical Engine**: a machine that could not only calculate, but could also be programmed using punch cards.

The Analytical Engine had everything a modern computer has:
- a "mill" (the processor)
- a "store" (the memory)
- input via punch cards
- output via a printer

Babbage never finished building it. The technology of his time wasn't precise enough. But the idea was there.

## 2. Ada Lovelace — the first programmer (1843)

Ada Lovelace was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron and a mathematically-minded mother.
She met Babbage when she was 17 and became fascinated by his machines.

In 1843 she translated an article about the Analytical Engine from French into English. But she added much more than a translation — she wrote extensive notes that made the original text three times as long.

In those notes, she described:
- how the machine could be used for more than just calculating
- how you could make the machine repeat the same instructions over and over (the first loop)
- a step-by-step plan for calculating Bernoulli numbers — the first algorithm ever written for a machine

But what truly set her apart was her insight: the machine processes *symbols*, not just numbers. She wrote that the Analytical Engine could even compose music — as long as you translate music into symbols.

That insight is the foundation of every modern computer.

> "The machine can only do what we order it to do."
> — Ada Lovelace

She died at the age of 36. Her work was only rediscovered a century later.

## 3. Alan Turing — what can a machine think? (1936)

Alan Turing was a British mathematician who asked a question that changed the world:

**What are the limits of what a machine can compute?**

In 1936 — before any computer existed — he described an imaginary machine: the **Turing machine**. An infinitely long tape with symbols, a read head that reads and writes symbols, and a set of rules that determines what happens next.

This abstract model could perform any computation that's possible to perform at all.
With it, Turing proved that there exist computations that *can never* be solved by a machine — no matter how powerful.

During the Second World War, Turing and his team cracked the Nazis' Enigma code — shortening the war by an estimated two years.

After the war, he asked the question that still occupies programmers today:

**Can a machine think?**

He designed the **Turing test**: if a human, communicating via text, cannot tell whether they're talking to a human or a machine, the machine has "passed" for intelligence.

Turing was prosecuted by the British government because of his homosexuality and died in 1954, likely by suicide. In 2013 he was granted a posthumous pardon by the British monarch.

## 4. The line from Babbage to now

```
Babbage (1820s)              → the idea: a machine that follows instructions
Lovelace (1843)               → the first algorithm, the first loop
Turing (1936)                 → the theory: what can a machine compute?
ENIAC (1945)                  → the first real electronic computer
transistor (1947)             → smaller, faster, cheaper
integrated circuit            → even smaller
personal computer             → in every living room
internet                      → connected
smartphone                    → in every pocket
AI (now)                      → machines that learn patterns themselves
```

Each step built on the previous one. And each step began with someone asking a question.

---

## 5. What does this mean for you?

You stand at the end of that line — and at the same time, at the beginning of something new.

The tools you're going to use are the result of eighty years of work by people who asked fascinating questions. Lovelace asked: what can a machine do besides calculate? Turing asked: what are the limits? The creators of Python asked: how do we write code that people can read?

As a developer, you'll ask your own questions. And the answers you write become part of that same line.
